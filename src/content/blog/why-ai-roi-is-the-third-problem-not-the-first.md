### **The most useful thing a prospect ever told me**

We were an hour into a proposal review with a defense litigation firm, ten to twelve attorneys, when the managing partner said something that reframed how I pitch this work.

He said, in effect: this is not going to grow our revenue.

He was not being difficult. He was being accurate. Payer bill review caps what any attorney in his firm can bill on a file. His top billers are already at that ceiling. And he was not going to reduce headcount, because he wants to grow. So the two levers people normally reach for when they justify software, bill more or employ fewer, were both closed to him.

Most vendors would treat that as an objection to overcome. I think it is the most honest starting point a firm can offer, and the pitch is better without the revenue story in it.

### **The order the value actually arrives in**

What that conversation clarified is that return on investment is not the first problem automation solves. It is the third. And it can only be collected after the first two are solved.

**Problem one is keeping your attorneys.**

This firm put it plainly. A new attorney takes roughly two years to reach break-even. Two years of supervision, of partner time spent correcting work, of files moving slower than they would in experienced hands. And then, having finally become profitable, that attorney leaves for five or ten thousand dollars more somewhere else.

Every one of those departures resets the clock. You are not losing a salary, you are losing the two-year investment that just started paying back.

**Problem two is how many attorneys one staffer can carry.**

The same firm described a paralegal who spends her day playing defense. Subpoena follow-up letters written by hand. Deadlines tracked in a system that reminds rather than acts. Emails that never make it into the digital file, so the file is never actually complete.

None of that is legal work. All of it consumes the capacity that determines how many attorneys she can support. If she can carry three, hiring a fourth attorney means hiring more staff. If she can carry five, it does not.

**Problem three is the return.**

Only once attorneys stay long enough to become profitable, and once each staffer can carry more of them, does the arithmetic start working in your favour. Attack problem three first and you are trying to collect on an investment that has not been made yet.

### **Why this matters for how you evaluate a proposal**

If you are looking at an automation quote and trying to model payback in billed hours, you will almost certainly talk yourself out of it. The number will not be there, because for most firms the billing ceiling is set by someone other than you.

The questions that actually predict whether the spend was worth it are duller and more useful:

- Does a second-year associate produce work a partner has to correct less often?
- Can a staffer support four attorneys where she used to support three?
- When a partner asks what is happening on a file, does anyone have to go and find out?

That last one came up more than any efficiency argument in our conversation. The managing partner's strongest personal draw was not saving time. It was being able to see what an associate had and had not done on a file without drilling through subfolders or interrupting the associate to ask.

That is not an ROI line item. It is the thing that makes the firm manageable, and manageable firms retain people.

### **The honest version of the business case**

So here is the case I would make, without the growth story.

Automation will not lift your billing ceiling. It will not let you take on cases you were otherwise unqualified for. What it does is remove the administrative drag that makes junior attorneys unprofitable for longer than they need to be, and that caps how many of them each staffer can support.

If that shortens the break-even on a new hire from twenty-four months to eighteen, the case makes itself, and it never once touches your revenue line.

The firms that get the most out of this work are the ones that stop asking what it will earn them and start asking what it will stop costing them.

### **A note on owning it**

One more thing from that call worth repeating. The partner nearing retirement raised a fair objection: AI will be cheaper in six to nine months, so why pay an entrance fee now and feel locked in?

The answer is not to argue about pricing curves. It is to not be locked in. If the firm owns the code, the workflows are yours, the data stays in your environment, and moving to a cheaper model when one ships is a maintenance task rather than a migration. The thing you are buying is your own process, encoded. The model underneath it is a component you should expect to swap.

If you are weighing the same decision, the useful question is not what it costs today. It is whether, in three years, you still own what you paid for.
