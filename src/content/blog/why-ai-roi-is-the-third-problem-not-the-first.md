**Return on investment is the third problem legal automation solves, not the first.** The first is keeping the attorneys you have long enough to become profitable. The second is raising how many attorneys each staff member can support. Only after both of those move does the arithmetic start working in your favour, which is why business cases built on billing more hours tend to collapse under inspection.

That order is not a theory. It came from a managing partner talking me out of my own pitch.

### **The most useful thing a prospect ever told me**

We were an hour into a proposal review with a defense litigation firm of ten to twelve attorneys when he said, in effect: this is not going to grow our revenue.

He was not being difficult. He was being accurate. Payer bill review caps what any attorney in his firm can bill on a file. His top billers were already at that ceiling. And he was not going to cut headcount, because he wants to grow. The two levers people normally reach for to justify software, bill more or employ fewer, were both closed to him.

Most vendors treat that as an objection to overcome. I think it is the most honest starting point a firm can offer, and the pitch is better without the revenue story in it.

### **Problem one: the attorneys you already paid to train**

The turnover numbers are worse than most partners realise, and worst at firms the size of his.

The [NALP Foundation's Update on Associate Attrition](https://www.nalpfoundation.org/news/the-nalp-foundation-releases-latest-update-on-associate-attrition-and-hiring-(cy-25)) put overall associate attrition at **19% in 2025**. At firms of 100 attorneys or fewer it was **24%**, materially higher than the 16% to 18% range at larger firms. Roughly one associate in four, every year, at exactly the firm size that can least absorb it.

Worse, they leave early. The same research found **83% of associates who departed in 2025 did so within five years of being hired**, up from 80% the year before, as reported by the [ABA Journal](https://www.abajournal.com/news/article/associates-continue-to-leave-firms-within-five-years-of-hire-report-says).

Put those together with what the partner told me. A new attorney at his firm takes about two years to reach break-even: two years of supervision, of partner time spent correcting work, of files moving slower than they would in experienced hands. Then, having finally turned profitable, that attorney leaves for five or ten thousand dollars more elsewhere.

You are not losing a salary when that happens. You are losing the two-year investment in the month it started paying back.

### **Problem two: how many attorneys one staffer can carry**

The same firm described a paralegal who spends her day playing defense. Subpoena follow-up letters written by hand. Deadlines in a system that reminds rather than acts. Emails that never reach the digital file, so the file is never actually complete.

None of that is legal work. All of it consumes the capacity that decides how many attorneys she can support. If she can carry three, hiring a fourth attorney means hiring more staff too. If she can carry five, it does not, and the economics of growing change entirely.

This is where document work sits. A single deposition runs two to three minutes a page to review by hand, and a medical record set can run to thousands of pages. Moving that to [AI deposition summaries](/deposition-summary-software/) and [medical chronologies cited to the source page](/ai-medical-record-summaries-chronologies/) does not make anyone bill more. It changes the ratio.

### **Problem three: the return**

Only once attorneys stay long enough to become profitable, and once each staffer carries more of them, does the money argument work. Attack problem three first and you are trying to collect on an investment nobody has made yet.

If you are looking at an automation quote and modelling payback in billed hours, you will probably talk yourself out of it. The number will not be there, because for most firms the billing ceiling is set by someone other than you.

The questions that actually predict whether the spend was worth it are duller:

- Does a second-year associate produce work a partner has to correct less often?
- Can a staffer support four attorneys where she used to support three?
- When a partner asks what is happening on a file, does anyone have to go and find out?

That last one came up more than any efficiency argument in our conversation. The managing partner's strongest personal draw was not saving time. It was seeing what an associate had and had not done on a file without drilling through subfolders or interrupting the associate to ask.

That is not an ROI line item. It is what makes a firm manageable, and manageable firms keep people.

### **The honest version of the business case**

Automation will not lift your billing ceiling. It will not qualify you for work you could not take before. What it removes is the administrative drag that keeps junior attorneys unprofitable longer than they need to be, and that caps how many of them each staffer can support.

If that shortens break-even on a new hire from twenty-four months to eighteen, at a firm losing roughly a quarter of its associates a year, the case makes itself. It never once touches your revenue line.

The firms that get the most from this work stop asking what it will earn them and start asking what it will stop costing them.

### **A note on owning it**

One more thing from that call. The partner nearest retirement raised a fair objection: AI will be cheaper in six to nine months, so why pay an entrance fee now and feel locked in?

The answer is not to argue about pricing curves. It is to not be locked in. When the firm owns the code, the workflows are yours, the data stays in your environment, and moving to a cheaper model when one ships is maintenance rather than migration. What you are buying is your own process, encoded. The model underneath is a component you should expect to swap. If you are weighing the same decision, the useful question is not what it costs today. It is whether, in three years, you still own what you paid for.

## FAQ

### Does legal AI increase a law firm's revenue?

Usually not directly, and firms should be sceptical of vendors who promise it. Where payer bill review or fee schedules cap what an attorney can bill on a file, no software raises that ceiling. The measurable gains show up in how quickly a new associate becomes profitable and how many attorneys each staff member can support.

### How long does a new associate take to become profitable?

It varies by practice, but two years is a common figure at small and mid-sized firms, counting supervision time, corrected work, and slower file movement. That matters because NALP found 83% of departing associates in 2025 left within five years of hire, so a firm often loses the investment shortly after it starts returning.

### What is a typical associate attrition rate?

The NALP Foundation reported 19% overall for 2025, rising to 24% at firms with 100 or fewer attorneys. Smaller firms carry the higher rate and have the least capacity to absorb the replacement cost.

### Should we cut staff after automating document work?

The firms that get the most from it generally do not. The value is in raising how many attorneys each existing staffer can support, which is what makes hiring the next attorney affordable. Cutting staff converts a growth lever into a one-off saving.

### What should we actually measure after implementing legal AI?

Three things, none of them billable hours: whether partners spend less time correcting junior work, whether the staff-to-attorney ratio improves, and whether file status is visible without someone having to go and find out. Those predict retention, which is where the return comes from.

### Do we own the workflows, or are we renting them?

Ask directly, and get it in writing. If the firm owns the code, the process is portable and swapping to a cheaper model later is a maintenance task. If it is rented per seat, the firm's own process lives inside someone else's product, and the exit cost grows with every workflow added.
